        How much to trust this

        • The numbers come straight from government agencies. The colors and sentences are my math on top; click any card to see it.
        • Colors are tested against 1960–2025. Each pop-up says how often trouble followed a reading like today's. That period had only 7 economic recessions, so every percentage rests on a handful of events.
        • Pandemic years set aside. The 2020 recession was a government shutdown, not a normal downturn, and 2020–21 relief money distorted the signals, so March 2019 to December 2021 is left out of the tests. The results barely changed, except that a flat yield curve looks less alarming over one year and more alarming over two.
        • Not tested: gold, credit card debt and company profits, because there isn't enough free history. Their colors are my judgment.
        • Patterns break. In 2022 the yield curve inverted, inflation hit 9% and mood collapsed, and no recession came.
        • A recession is not the same as a stock crash. Stocks often fall before recessions and recover during them. This page is about the economy, not when to buy or sell. Not financial advice.

        What this means: —

        Remember
        • M2 is checking, savings and cash: money that is easy to spend. Most new money is created when banks lend, not printed. Unused credit card limits are not money; the moment you swipe, the store gets a new deposit, and that deposit is.
        • Inflation only hurts when paychecks fall behind it. That is why the inflation card also checks pay and jobs.
        • In 2020 money jumped while factories and shipping were shut. More money chasing fewer goods meant higher prices about a year and a half later.
        • This is a pattern, not a law. Use it as an early warning, then look for confirmation in the inflation numbers.

        The brake pedal and the warning light

        The Fed sets the short-term rate. Markets set the 10-year. When short rates sit above long rates, the curve is "inverted", a classic recession warning.

        What this means: —

        Yield curve: 10-year minus 2-year

        Percentage points · red bars are inversions

        What this means: —

        Remember
        • The 10-year rate after inflation is what a safe 10-year government bond pays you beyond keeping up with prices. It is the no-risk reward everything else has to beat.
        • Real rate = interest rate minus inflation. Above zero, the Fed is pressing the brake. Below zero, borrowing is basically free money.
        • Mortgages follow the 10-year more than the Fed's rate, which is why mortgage rates can rise while the Fed cuts.
        • The curve inverted in 2022 and was widely called a recession signal. Stocks rose strongly in 2023 and 2024. That is your "obvious crash" test.

        Remember
        • Gold pays nothing. You give up interest to hold it, so it usually does worse when real rates are high.
        • When it rises anyway, the driver is fear and central banks buying gold, not rates.
        • History: the US made people turn in gold in 1933 and cut the last dollar-gold link in 1971. Since then, the dollar's value rests on trust and on taxes being payable only in dollars.

        What this means: —

        Remember
        • Lipstick effect: in tough times people cut big purchases but keep small treats. Watch restaurants, beauty and clothing against groceries.
        • Low sentiment with strong spending is a mismatch. Either moods recover, or spending eventually follows moods down.
        • A falling saving rate plus rising card debt means spending is being stretched.
        • The health and beauty store figures drop by about 8% in one month in January 2026, likely a data revision, so treat that row with caution.

        Is profit real?

        Profit is an accounting number; cash flow is money that actually came in. If profits climb much faster than cash, something on paper is doing the work.

        US company profits per $1 of cash flow

        All US corporations, after tax · quarterly · dashed line is the 2015–2024 average

        What this means: —

        Remember
        • Cash flow is always bigger because it adds back depreciation (the paper cost of equipment wearing out). The ratio's trend matters, not its level.
        • The 2017 spike is the tax-law change, not fraud: one-time tax charges cut cash in that quarter.
